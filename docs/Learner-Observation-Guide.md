# Learner Observation Guide

The first five missions are experiments. The most useful output is not only the learner's answers, but observations about how the learner thinks.

## What to observe

While the learner works, note:

### Understanding
- Can the learner restate what the problem is asking?
- Does the learner confuse important details with irrelevant ones?
- Does the learner know what the desired end state is?

### Finding a foothold
- Does the learner identify any useful known fact without prompting?
- What kind of facts feel naturally useful?
- Does the learner freeze when no method is obvious?

### Building a path
- Does the learner try something independently?
- Does the learner draw, write, count, make a timeline, or invent another representation?
- Does the learner explain why a step might help?

### Productive struggle
- How long does uncertainty remain productive?
- At what point does it turn into frustration or paralysis?
- Which hint restores movement?
- Was any hint too revealing?

### Flexibility
- If one route fails, does the learner try another?
- Can the learner return to a useful intermediate point instead of restarting everything?
- After solving, can the learner understand an alternate path?

### Checking
- Does the learner test whether an answer is reasonable?
- Does the learner notice contradictions?
- Does the learner revise without treating revision as failure?

### Confidence and emotion
- Which moments produce visible satisfaction?
- Which wording feels encouraging versus school-like?
- Does the mascot help?
- Does gamification feel motivating, distracting, or childish?

## What the grown-up should avoid

During the prototype sessions, try not to:
- name the arithmetic operation too early;
- turn a hint into a mini-lesson;
- rescue immediately when the learner hesitates;
- praise only correctness;
- imply that the intended method is the only good method.

Instead, prompts can include:
- “What do you know for sure?”
- “What might be useful?”
- “What could you try?”
- “What happened when you tried that?”
- “Did that get us closer?”
- “Where was the last point that still made sense?”
- “Is there another way you could look at it?”

## Revision rule

Do not redesign from one isolated moment unless it reveals a clear structural problem.

Look for patterns across several missions:
- repeated confusion;
- repeated dependence on the same hint;
- too much or too little writing space;
- consistent avoidance of a section;
- unexpectedly strong engagement with a particular interaction.

Those patterns should drive the eventual master template.
