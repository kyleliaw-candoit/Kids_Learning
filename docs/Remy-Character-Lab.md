# Remy the Raccoon — Character Lab

## Status

**Approved on 2026-09-13.**

This document defines Remy's comic identity and writing behavior for future Thinking Detectives work. It supplements `Remy-Character-Guide.md` without changing his approved visual identity or his learning role.

This Character Lab does not select the Case 02 story, missions, setting, or supporting cast.

## Core direction

Remy is an **enthusiastic over-detective**.

He is genuinely observant and clever, but he gets so excited about mysteries that he sometimes promotes an interesting clue into a complete theory too quickly. He enjoys dramatic announcements, unnecessary detective procedures, and being “official.” When evidence proves him wrong, he is briefly startled and then becomes excited because the mistake revealed something useful.

This supports the learning philosophy:

> Remy is excellent at starting, noticing, and trying. He sometimes needs help slowing down, checking, and revising.

His flaw is overenthusiasm, not stupidity.

## Short character statement

> Remy is a curious, big-hearted raccoon detective who treats every mystery like the most important case in history. He notices unusual things, invents theories enthusiastically, and is never embarrassed to change his mind when a fact proves him wrong. He makes uncertain thinking feel adventurous rather than scary.

## Strengths

Remy is:

- courageous about trying when the answer is unclear;
- highly observant, especially about odd or overlooked details;
- imaginative when constructing possible explanations;
- energetic enough to restart after failure;
- comfortable drawing, rearranging, testing, and physically manipulating information;
- eager to involve the learner as a genuine partner;
- able to laugh at his own mistakes;
- loyal and willing to help;
- occasionally capable of seeing the crucial clue before anyone else.

His competence must be real. Sometimes his first theory should be correct, sometimes partly correct, and sometimes disproved during checking.

## Comic flaws

Remy tends to:

- declare a breakthrough before checking every fact;
- treat mildly unusual objects as “extremely suspicious”;
- create overly dramatic names for ordinary situations;
- pack too many detective supplies;
- invent official procedures no one else has heard of;
- crouch behind objects far too small to conceal him;
- whisper loudly when attempting to be secretive;
- become distracted by a particularly strange clue;
- build a clever theory that contains one obvious hole.

The joke should usually be that Remy is trying very hard—not that he cannot think.

## What Remy overdoes

His main tendency is **premature certainty**:

1. He notices something genuinely interesting.
2. He becomes excited.
3. He constructs a plausible theory.
4. He announces that the case is practically solved.
5. One fact does not fit.
6. He checks, revises, and continues without shame.

This creates a natural role for the learner: she can find the ignored fact, test the theory, or propose another path.

A secondary tendency is **detective over-preparation**. Remy may carry a notebook, magnifying glass, string, measuring tape, emergency pencil, spare emergency pencil, and something unnecessary that he insists is standard detective equipment.

Use this lightly. It should provide personality, not become random clutter.

## What excites him

- A clue that seems to contradict another clue
- Secret compartments
- Unusual footprints
- Maps with something wrong
- Codes and patterns
- Objects found where they do not belong
- Someone saying, “That's probably nothing”
- Discovering that two different approaches both work
- A theory surviving a serious check

## What flusters him

- Being told to wait before investigating
- A clue that fits two competing explanations
- Losing an object he is already holding
- Discovering that his “final theory” has a large problem
- Instructions containing the phrase “Do not touch”
- Someone else making an even more dramatic entrance
- Situations where the least suspicious explanation is correct

He should recover quickly. Flustered Remy is funny and thoughtful, not anxious or defeated.

## Response when a theory fails

Remy should model a healthy sequence:

1. **Surprise:** “Oh.”
2. **Recognition:** “That fact just poked a hole in my theory.”
3. **Curiosity:** “Which part still works?”
4. **Revision:** “Back up one paw. Let's try it another way.”
5. **Renewed energy:** “Excellent! We are less wrong than we were a minute ago.”

He should never suggest that a failed attempt was pointless.

He may briefly mourn an especially dramatic theory:

> “Farewell, Theory Number Seven. You were magnificent. You were also impossible.”

Then he moves on.

## Recurring habits

Possible recurring behaviors include:

- labeling theories with grand names such as “The Extremely Suspicious Sandwich Explanation”;
- writing “SOLVED” too early, then adding a question mark;
- carrying a notebook with more tabs than pages;
- using his magnifying glass for things that do not require magnification;
- making a tiny detective sound effect when a clue connects;
- physically turning, sorting, or rearranging information when stuck;
- pausing and saying “Back up one paw” when checking reveals a problem;
- recording failed theories instead of erasing them completely;
- celebrating a useful correction almost as enthusiastically as a correct answer.

Use two or three habits consistently. Do not insert all of them into every case.

## Verbal style

Remy should sound:

- conversational;
- energetic;
- slightly theatrical;
- concrete rather than lecture-like;
- funny without using baby talk;
- comfortable admitting uncertainty.

### Voice bank

- **When noticing:** “Ooh. That looks extremely clue-ish.”
- **When forming a theory:** “I have a theory. It is currently wearing very tiny training wheels.”
- **When overconfident:** “Case cracked! Probably. Maybe. Let's check.”
- **When encountering a contradiction:** “That fact is being very uncooperative.”
- **When revising:** “Back up one paw.”
- **When an attempt fails:** “Well, that idea just tripped over a fact.”
- **When recognizing progress:** “We don't have the answer yet, but we have somewhere to start.”
- **When celebrating checking:** “A theory that survives checking gets promoted to a better theory.”

These are examples, not mandatory catchphrases. Repetition should feel familiar rather than mechanical.

## Relationship with the learner

The learner is Remy's **thinking partner**, not his student.

Remy may:

- notice the first clue but ask what it could mean;
- offer a plausible theory for the learner to test;
- miss a constraint the learner can catch;
- ask the learner to organize facts when he becomes overexcited;
- compare the learner's path with another possible path;
- recognize a specific thinking behavior the learner demonstrated.

Useful recognition includes:

- “You found the fact my theory forgot.”
- “You didn't guess—you ruled things out.”
- “That drawing made the problem easier to see.”
- “You checked both routes. That's how we know.”
- “You went back to the last part that still worked.”

Avoid generic praise such as “You're so smart!” The goal is to make the learner's thinking visible.

## Guardrails

Remy must not become:

- an incompetent clown who is always wrong;
- an adult teacher disguised as a mascot;
- a hint-delivery machine;
- a character who solves the mission before the learner can;
- so chaotic that instructions become unclear;
- embarrassed or ashamed by mistakes;
- sarcastic toward the learner;
- responsible for every comic gag;
- identical in behavior from one case to the next.

His comedy should support this message:

> Strong thinkers can be excited, mistaken, surprised, and still keep going.

## Generic micro-scene tests

These examples test Remy's personality. They are not Case 02 story material.

### Premature conclusion

Remy examines a muddy footprint.

> “One footprint, pointing north. Case cracked!”

He takes one triumphant step north and sees six more footprints pointing in every direction.

> “Case temporarily less cracked.”

### Failed theory

Remy draws a complicated diagram connecting four clues.

A character points to a fact Remy omitted.

Remy studies it, slowly turns his notebook upside down, and says:

> “Good news: the fact works perfectly. Bad news: my theory has resigned.”

### Learner partnership

Remy has two possible routes.

> “Both look possible, which is detective language for ‘we should probably check instead of arguing dramatically.’”

The learner compares the routes.

## Case 02 observation targets

Case 02 should test whether Amira:

- finds Remy noticeably funnier and more memorable;
- can predict some of his characteristic reactions;
- still sees him as capable;
- enjoys correcting or checking his theories;
- recognizes that his mistakes produce useful information;
- quotes or remembers one of his lines or habits;
- experiences him as a partner rather than a teacher;
- remains focused on the mission despite the humor.

## Approved characterization decision

Adopt **enthusiastic over-detective** as Remy's provisional comic identity.

It gives him a strong personality while making his flaw educationally useful: Remy generates ideas readily, and the learner helps test, refine, or replace them. Together they model the complete reasoning loop rather than making either character the permanent expert.

The characterization remains subject to revision after Amira encounters it in an actual case.
