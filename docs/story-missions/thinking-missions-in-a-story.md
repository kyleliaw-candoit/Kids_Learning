# Thinking Missions in a Story

An engaging way to build real thinking skills through story-based adventures.

## 1. Overview

**Thinking Missions in a Story** is the core experience design for story-integrated Thinking Detectives cases.

Each chapter is a short, self-contained story with several embedded Thinking Missions. The child progresses through the story by solving each mission, and each completed mission unlocks the next meaningful part of the story.

Along the way, the child may also earn Thinking Badges that reflect the kinds of thinking they used.

> **It is not about always knowing the answer. It is about knowing what to do when you do not know what to do.**

## 2. How it works

A typical chapter follows this rhythm:

**Story Part 1 → Mission 1 → Story Part 2 → Mission 2 → Story Part 3 → Mission 3 → Story Part 4 → Mission 4 → Story Part 5 → Mission 5 → Finale**

Each mission should sit at a natural suspense point where the child’s thinking helps the characters overcome a real obstacle, interpret a clue, make a plan, or unlock a discovery.

Five missions is the current prototype target. Four to six may be appropriate depending on story rhythm.

## 3. Why use a story layer?

### Engaging
The story creates curiosity and emotional investment in what happens next.

### Purposeful
Each mission practices a specific thinking capability in a context that feels meaningful rather than worksheet-like.

### Motivating
Completing a mission earns an intrinsically interesting reward: the next clue, scene, twist, or reveal. Thinking Badges provide a complementary layer of recognition.

### Transferable
The story shows that thinking skills are useful in real, interesting, messy situations—not only in school exercises.

## 4. Mission design principles

1. **Anchored in the story when practical** — connect the problem naturally to the plot, setting, clue, or character.
2. **One Primary Thinking Dimension** — every mission has exactly one main reasoning capability it is intentionally designed to exercise.
3. **Up to two Secondary Thinking Dimensions** — supporting dimensions should reflect real cognitive work, not loose associations.
4. **Age-appropriate and accessible** — challenging enough to require thought, but solvable with persistence and a progressive hint ladder.
5. **Multiple valid approaches when possible** — avoid implying that every problem has one hidden teacher-approved method.
6. **Built-in hint ladder** — preserve productive struggle while preventing total paralysis.
7. **Meaningful story reward** — solving the mission must unlock something worth discovering.
8. **Path before answer** — the learner’s reasoning process remains more important than simply reaching the final answer quickly.

## 5. Story and education are two cooperating layers

### Education layer
Determines:
- Primary and Secondary Thinking Dimensions;
- problem difficulty;
- productive struggle;
- hint ladder;
- multiple solution paths;
- checking and reflection.

### Story layer
Determines:
- characters;
- mystery/adventure;
- suspense;
- humor;
- clue order;
- twists and reveals;
- finale.

The layers should support each other, but **thinking quality takes priority over story fit**. A strong reasoning mission should not be weakened merely to make it perfectly thematic.

## 6. Short cases, not one giant novel

Each case should normally have its own beginning, central mystery/adventure, reveal, and ending.

This keeps the system:
- easier to create and test;
- easy to pause and resume;
- flexible across genres and settings;
- responsive to what is learned from the previous case;
- satisfying because the child receives a complete payoff after only a handful of missions.

Recurring characters and a loose shared world are welcome, but long continuity should not become a production burden.

## 7. Thinking Badges

Story progression is the primary reward. Badges make invisible thinking behaviors visible.

Examples:
- **Clue Finder** — noticed something useful;
- **Path Builder** — constructed a way to start;
- **Brave Try** — tried an idea before being certain;
- **Check-It** — tested whether an answer or step made sense;
- **Try Again** — revised an approach and continued.

Badges should never become a punitive score based on correctness.

## 8. The story should model good thinking

Remy and other recurring characters should sometimes:
- get confused;
- make reasonable wrong guesses;
- take inefficient paths;
- notice contradictions;
- revise their thinking;
- ask for help;
- laugh at mistakes;
- celebrate finding a foothold.

The child should see that strong thinkers are not people who instantly know everything. Strong thinkers know how to keep moving.

## 9. Prototype

**Case 01 — The Mystery of the Upside-Down Map** is the first story-integrated prototype.

Its five Primary Thinking Dimensions are:

1. Representation & Model Building
2. Working Backward
3. Decomposition
4. Elimination & Constraint Reasoning
5. Sequencing & Planning

See `case-01-upside-down-map-prototype-spec.md` for the full story and mission design.

## 10. What to observe during testing

The system remains experimental. Watch for:
- whether the child genuinely wants to continue the story;
- whether story passages are too long or too short;
- whether curiosity causes rushing through the reasoning;
- whether missions feel naturally connected to the plot;
- whether badges add motivation or clutter;
- whether roughly five missions feels right for one case;
- whether the finale provides enough payoff.

Future structure should be revised from real learner behavior rather than standardized prematurely.
