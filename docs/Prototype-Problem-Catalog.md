# Prototype Problem Catalog

The first five problems intentionally sample different reasoning moves rather than five versions of the same math skill.

## Problem 1 — Time Difference

**Primary reasoning move:** finding anchor facts / building a route between two points in time.

Example task: determine how much time passes from 3:30 p.m. to 5:15 p.m.

Possible paths include:
- move from 3:30 to 4:00, then through a whole hour, then to 5:15;
- notice that 3:30 to 5:30 is two hours, then remove 15 minutes;
- use another equivalent timeline decomposition.

Why it was chosen: it reveals whether the learner can construct a method from known time anchors instead of simply recognizing “this is subtraction.”

## Problem 2 — Working Backward

**Primary reasoning move:** reconstructing the beginning from a known ending state.

Prototype structure: an item costs $8 and $12 remains; determine the starting amount.

Why it was chosen: the learner must reason about what happened and mentally reverse it instead of relying only on the surface order of the numbers.

## Problem 3 — Break Into Parts

**Primary reasoning move:** decomposition and recombination.

Prototype structure: a route contains several small segments; determine the total distance traveled.

Why it was chosen: the arithmetic is intentionally easy so attention can go toward identifying pieces of a whole and deciding how to combine them.

## Problem 4 — Sequencing / Planning

**Primary reasoning move:** organize tasks and test whether a plan fits a constraint.

Prototype task: at 4:10 p.m., complete 20 minutes of homework and 15 minutes of cleanup before 5:00 p.m.

Possible paths include:
- add task durations and compare with available time;
- build a forward timeline;
- work backward from the deadline.

Why it was chosen: it resembles a real planning decision rather than a school-style operation prompt.

## Problem 5 — Logic Elimination

**Primary reasoning move:** use facts to rule out impossible options.

Prototype structure: three people have different pets/items; clues identify or eliminate possibilities.

Why it was chosen: it removes arithmetic almost entirely and tests whether the same “what do I know, what does that rule out, what follows next?” habit transfers to a pure logic setting.

## Difficulty philosophy

The initial set aims for:
- **light computation**;
- **moderately challenging reasoning**;
- enough structure to support success;
- enough uncertainty to require construction of a path.

The problems should not all feel equally difficult. Observation of the learner will determine whether this balance needs adjustment.
